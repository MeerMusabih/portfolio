# FlyRank AI Internship — Retrospective

When I started the FlyRank AI internship, my main goal was to become more comfortable building practical AI and machine-learning systems rather than only studying the concepts theoretically. I wanted to understand how AI could be turned into something that actually works from input to output, including data preparation, model evaluation, automation, APIs, and deployment. I also wanted to become more confident working with modern AI tools and understanding where they are useful and where they have limitations.

Over the course of the internship, that goal became much more practical. Instead of thinking about an AI system as simply a model that produces an answer, I learned to think about the complete system around it. My work included machine-learning modeling, AI workflows, automation, local language models, API integration, evaluation, debugging, documentation, and demonstrations.

One of the most important changes was learning to treat evaluation and reliability as part of the build rather than something added at the end. In my machine-learning work, I worked with a content-refresh dataset and built a decision-support model for identifying pages that may need refreshing. I compared a rule-based baseline against a Random Forest model and evaluated the ranking quality using Precision@20 and Precision@50. This helped me understand that a model should be judged against a meaningful baseline and evaluated using metrics that match the actual task.

I also built the FL-07 Job Finder Agent, which became one of the clearest examples of how my approach changed. The agent accepts natural-language job preferences, uses a local Ollama model to interpret them, searches live job sources, combines and deduplicates listings, pre-ranks candidates, evaluates shortlisted jobs individually, applies ranking and seniority guardrails, and returns structured results. During development, I encountered a problem where evaluating multiple jobs in one LLM prompt produced repetitive and poorly differentiated results. Instead of simply accepting the output, I tested the behavior, identified the failure mode, and changed the architecture so that jobs were evaluated individually. That experience taught me that building with AI requires testing the actual behavior rather than assuming the model will behave as expected.

Another major lesson was learning to work with AI as a development partner while still taking responsibility for the final result. I used AI tools such as Claude and ChatGPT for workflow design, debugging, implementation support, and documentation. However, I had to test the generated suggestions, inspect errors, run the workflows, and make the final engineering decisions. This changed how I think about AI-assisted development: the goal is not to have AI do everything, but to use it to move faster while remaining responsible for correctness.

The three most transferable things I learned are **system thinking, evaluation, and iterative debugging**. First, I learned to think about the entire pipeline instead of focusing only on an individual model or component. Second, I learned that evaluation should be designed around the actual objective and compared with a baseline. Third, I learned that failures are useful engineering information: testing an imperfect system, identifying why it fails, and changing the design is often more valuable than simply getting a successful first run.

If I continued for another month, I would improve the Job Finder Agent with persistent job history, scheduled searches, notifications, more job sources, and a larger evaluation dataset. I would also experiment with a stronger model and measure whether the improvement in matching quality justified the additional compute or cost.

Looking back at Week 1, the biggest difference is that I now have more experience moving from an idea to a working AI system and then explaining its design, evaluation, and limitations honestly. The internship helped me become more comfortable not just using AI, but engineering with it.
